# Actors Finding — Shared, Collective and Institutional Agency

**Status:** current topic finding  
**Scope:** Actors-local distinctions; not a theory that all groups or organizations are agents

## Current finding

Plurality alone does not create a collective Actor. The research also found that two different non-individual phenomena must be kept apart:

1. **shared/joint agency** — several individuals act together through appropriately related participatory intentions/commitments without requiring a separate supra-individual agent;
2. **corporate/group agency** — a sufficiently organized collective supports group-level attitudes, decisions or actions through organizational structure and decision procedures.

Neither is accurately represented as a mere count or sum of individual behavior, but they are not the same phenomenon.

## Distinctions to preserve

### 1. Mere aggregation ≠ shared/joint action

Shared-agency literature uses cases in which outwardly similar individual behavior differs depending on whether participants are intentionally and normatively coordinated. Several people independently doing similar things need not constitute shared action.

### 2. Shared/joint agency ≠ supra-individual group agent

Individuals can act together without assuming that every temporary `we` is a separate enduring subject with its own mind, identity or personhood. Shared agency can be analysed through interrelated participatory intentions and commitments among individuals.

This is an important counterexample to `collective action implies collective entity`.

### 3. Collection ≠ organized corporate/group agent

Christian List distinguishes aggregate, common and corporate attitudes. Aggregate and common attitudes can be attributed to a collection without treating it as a group agent; corporate attitudes depend on organizational structure and decision procedures and carry the stronger group-agency commitment.

### 4. Group-level attitude/action ≠ majority member attitude

A corporate attitude may be produced by an organizational decision procedure and diverge from the attitudes of most individual members. `The organization decided X` therefore cannot safely be translated into `most members wanted X`.

### 5. Organizational action can be realized through individuals without being identical to one individual's action

Collectives generally act through members, representatives, offices or systems. That implementation fact does not by itself settle the level at which a decision or action is institutionally attributed.

### 6. Group responsibility ≠ distributed individual responsibility

Collective responsibility literature allows genuinely collective responsibility while also allowing individual members to bear separate responsibility. The two levels need not be mutually exclusive or numerically identical.

### 7. Decision procedure and organizational structure are semantically important

If a group-level action or attitude depends on voting, quorum, authorization chain, office structure, deliberation or another procedure, the procedure is not incidental metadata. It can determine whether a corporate attribution is warranted and whose participation matters.

## Decision-critical implication

The collective-reduction assumption is rejected, but so are two opposite shortcuts:

- every coordinated plurality is a group agent;
- every named organization is automatically a first-class agent in every semantic sense.

A later synthesis must be able to ask:

- are the people merely behaving in parallel or participating in a shared action;
- if shared, what commitments/relations make the activity joint;
- is there also an organized corporate bearer with decision procedures;
- what organizational/decision structure licenses a group-level attribution;
- which members, offices or representatives participated and how;
- whether the statement is aggregate, common, shared/joint or corporate in character;
- whether group-level and individual-level responsibility coexist.

The research does not prescribe this literature's vocabulary as the SOUL model; it uses the distinctions to falsify naive collapse.

## Evidence used

### Stanford Encyclopedia of Philosophy — Shared Agency

Inspected view:
- web entry on shared agency, including the distinction between mere aggregation and joint activity, interrelated participatory intentions, and the debate over whether shared activity requires a supra-individual subject.

Source:
- https://plato.stanford.edu/entries/shared-agency/

Supports:
- joint/shared action is not reducible to outward behavioral aggregation;
- shared action need not automatically imply a separate enduring group agent.

Use limitation:
- philosophical accounts of shared intention are contested; no single theory is adopted as SOUL truth.

### Christian List — `Three Kinds of Collective Attitudes`

Inspected view:
- author's LSE-hosted PDF, including abstract, distinction among aggregate/common/corporate attitudes, and later discussion of organizational generation and divergence from majority member attitudes.
- page-level screenshots were inspected for the relevant sections.

Source:
- https://personal.lse.ac.uk/list/PDF-files/CollectiveAttitudes.pdf

Supports:
- not every collective attitude implies group agency;
- organized corporate attitudes can be action-guiding and differ from member-majority attitudes;
- organizational structure/decision procedures matter.

Does not establish:
- that every organization is a moral or legal person;
- a universal threshold for group agency.

## Residual uncertainty

- The minimum integration conditions for shared action and the minimum organizational conditions for group agency are contested and domain-sensitive.
- Legal personality, moral agency, operational group agency, shared agency and institutional decision attribution must not be assumed coextensive.
- Succession, merger, reorganization and changing membership create continuity questions that belong to later modelling or domain-specific research unless they become decision-critical in synthesis.
